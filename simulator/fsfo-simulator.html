How to read this

Seven things the timeline is telling you

The threshold is a reconnect budget

FastStartFailoverThreshold is not a timeout on one ping: it is how long the master observer keeps trying to reconnect, counted from the moment it first lost the connection. That moment is t = 0 here.

Two parties have to agree

Fast-start failover requires the observer and the target standby to have both lost the primary for the threshold. During the countdown the observer confirms the standby has also lost contact and is ready; if the standby can still see the primary, no failover.

Readiness means different things per mode

In MaxAvailability and MaxProtection the standby must have been synchronized at failure time. In MaxPerformance its apply lag must be at or below FastStartFailoverLagLimit. Fail the check and failover is not allowed at all.

Execution is not a parameter

Once initiated, the standby applies the redo it already holds, converts to the primary role, and opens read-write. That span depends on the apply lag at failure time and on your environment; no broker property shortens it.

The ping properties override the threshold

Per the broker documentation, FastStartFailoverThreshold is ignored when ObserverPingRetry and ObserverPingInterval are set. Detection then costs interval × (retries + 1), about a second at the minimums.

An isolated primary runs its own clock

Cut off from both the observer and the standby, the primary stalls — V$DATABASE.FS_FAILOVER_STATUS goes to STALLED — and, if FastStartFailoverPmyShutdown is TRUE, aborts itself after one threshold. Two clocks, one open primary.

No observer, no fast-start failover

Lose the only observer and nothing visible breaks: the primary still commits, the standby still applies. But V$DATABASE.FS_FAILOVER_STATUS now reads PRIMARY UNOBSERVED, FS_FAILOVER_OBSERVER_PRESENT reads NO, and the broker raises ORA-16820. 19c registers up to three observers; lose the master while primary and standby stay connected and the broker promotes a backup on its own.

Transport layer · one commit

What one commit pays for the standby — SYNC, FASTSYNC, ASYNC

The failure clocks above run only when something breaks; the transport cost is paid in milliseconds on every commit, for as long as the configuration runs. The broker property is LogXptMode — SYNC, FASTSYNC or ASYNC — and what separates the three is one disk write on the standby and one round trip on the wire.

AFFIRM is a disk promise

Each LogXptMode value sets a pair of destination attributes. SYNC means SYNC + AFFIRM: the destination acknowledges received redo only after writing it to the standby redo log. FASTSYNC means SYNC + NOAFFIRM: it acknowledges before that write. Under both, a transaction cannot commit until the destination has acknowledged all its redo; the two differ only in what the acknowledgement is worth. All three values require standby redo logs.

FASTSYNC trades the disk wait for time

FASTSYNC exists since 12.1, in maximum availability mode only. It buys back the standby redo log write, but only while the network is the bottleneck: once the local write outlasts the round trip, the chip above reads zero. The cost is a window in which the standby has acknowledged redo it has not yet written; the broker documents that fast-start failover cannot be initiated when FASTSYNC is in use and the standby is missing redo data.

ASYNC answers before the standby knows

Under ASYNC + NOAFFIRM, the broker default, a transaction never waits for the destination: the commit costs the local write alone. Redo still on the wire is the exposure, which is why MaxPerformance gates fast-start failover on FastStartFailoverLagLimit rather than on synchronization. The broker’s protection-mode table lists ASYNC for MaxPerformance, and SYNC or FASTSYNC for MaxAvailability. MaxProtection is out of scope here.

Transport layer

Where NET_TIMEOUT fits — and where it doesn't

NET_TIMEOUT is an attribute of LOG_ARCHIVE_DEST_n on the primary (broker property NetTimeout), not a failover clock: it caps how many seconds LGWR blocks waiting for the standby to acknowledge synchronously shipped redo, after which an error is logged, the transport session is terminated, and the destination is dropped. Neither the observer nor the target standby consults it. It and FastStartFailoverThreshold both default to 30 seconds, which is why the two get confused, so both panels span the same window and the brace under each shows which clock is really running. Panel B shortens NET_TIMEOUT to 10 s — the configuration that looks like it should lose data — to show why it doesn't.

“NET_TIMEOUT 10 s, threshold 30 s — do I lose 20 s of redo?”

No. The worry assumes the primary commits alone from second 10 and the failover at second 30 discards those commits. But NET_TIMEOUT expiring only brings the primary to a decision point; whether it may continue without its standby is a separate check, and an isolated primary fails it.

Continuing alone requires a witness

With fast-start failover enabled, a MaxAvailability primary may drop to UNSYNCHRONIZED and keep committing only while it can still reach the observer or the target standby. In panel A the observer is reachable, so the primary carries on and fast-start failover is disarmed until the standby resynchronizes. In panel B nobody answers, so redo generation stalls: V$DATABASE.FS_FAILOVER_STATUS goes to STALLED.

Why the failover still loses nothing

Under SYNC a commit is not acknowledged until the standby holds its redo, so every transaction a client ever saw succeed is already on the standby when it opens as the new primary. Commits issued during the stall never return: those sessions hang until the old primary aborts and get errors, not silent loss. A shorter NET_TIMEOUT only moves the moment the hanging starts.

In MaxPerformance (ASYNC) commits never wait for the standby, so NET_TIMEOUT never touches user sessions and never appears on the failover timeline; eligibility and data loss are bounded by FastStartFailoverLagLimit instead.

Transport layer · after the drop

The destination is dropped — ReopenSecs and MaxFailure decide when redo flows again

This panel picks up where panel A stops: the error is logged, the transport session is terminated, and the standby destination LOG_ARCHIVE_DEST_2 is closed. The gap is at least ReopenSecs

Nothing brings a failed destination back sooner than one ReopenSecs, however brief the outage. The property gates eligibility; the log switch supplies the trigger: transport retries a failed destination at log switch time, once the last error plus the REOPEN interval is in the past. The real retry therefore lands at the first log switch after the interval expires, which is why a busy database recovers faster than a quiet one. REOPEN applies to every error, not only connection failures.

MaxFailure is a tripwire, not a retry budget

MaxFailure counts consecutive attempts, at log switches, to reestablish a failed destination; its default 0 means unlimited attempts. Reaching the limit does not slow the retries down — it disables the destination: no further access until it is manually reenabled, and it is then treated as if REOPEN had not been specified. The network healing afterwards changes nothing. One documented exception: destinations with the 12.2 GROUP and PRIORITY attributes re-enter service on their own once redo transport finds them accessible.

This is where the lag limit expires

Nothing is arriving, so the standby falls behind in real time. In MaxAvailability it is UNSYNCHRONIZED from the first second and fast-start failover is disarmed immediately; in MaxPerformance it survives until the apply lag passes FastStartFailoverLagLimit — 30 s by default, half a minute into an outage that may run for hours. Either way, no failover is permitted until the destination is shipping and the standby is back in step.

The service name is the switch

A role-based service exists only where the database currently runs as primary (srvctl add service -role PRIMARY, or a startup trigger checking DATABASE_ROLE). The standby’s listener is up and answers immediately with ORA-12514, because nothing publishes app_svc there. That instant refusal makes a two-address string safe: the wrong database can never accept the connection.

The dead address is a toll booth

With LOAD_BALANCE off the client walks the list in order, so every traversal first pays min(TRANSPORT_CONNECT_TIMEOUT, CONNECT_TIMEOUT) on the unreachable host — including every fresh connection after the failover, until the old primary is reinstated or the string reordered. LOAD_BALANCE = ON randomizes the order and halves the expected toll.

The default string retries exactly never

RETRY_COUNT defaults to 0: one traversal, then the error goes straight to the application. The budget must cover the whole outage: (RETRY_COUNT + 1) × traversal + RETRY_COUNT × RETRY_DELAY has to reach past the new primary’s open, with room to spare.

Short delays, many retries

Oracle’s HA guidance: a generous CONNECT_TIMEOUT (90 s in the documented example), a transport timeout around a second, and a short RETRY_DELAY with a high RETRY_COUNT, so that one of the traversals lands just after the service is republished. Its switchover sizing example budgets 100 × 3 s.

These parameters govern only the dial tone

Nothing here touches sessions that were already connected. They die by TCP rules — instant resets from a reachable host, long hangs across a partition — unless keepalives, SQLNET.RECV_TIMEOUT, or FAN break them faster; TAF or Application Continuity resumes their work afterwards. This panel models new connections only.

When no failover is coming

If the readiness check above refuses the failover, the service is never republished; every traversal ends in ORA-12514, the string exhausts its budget, and the application stays down until a human intervenes. Retry parameters buy time, not availability.
