How to read this

Six things the timeline is telling you

The threshold is a reconnect budget

FastStartFailoverThreshold is not a timeout on one ping. It is the number of seconds the master observer keeps attempting to reconnect to the primary, counted from the moment it first lost the connection. That moment is t = 0 here.

Two parties have to agree

Fast-start failover happens when the observer and the target standby have both lost the primary for the threshold. During the countdown the observer confirms with the standby that it also lost contact and is ready. If the standby still has contact with the primary, no failover.

Readiness means different things per mode

In MaxAvailability and MaxProtection the standby must have been synchronized at failure time. In MaxPerformance its apply lag must be at or below FastStartFailoverLagLimit. Fail that check and fast-start failover is not allowed at all.

Execution is not a parameter

Once initiated, the standby finishes applying the redo it already received, converts to the primary role, and opens read-write. That span grows with the apply lag at failure time and with your environment. No broker property shortens it.

The ping properties override the threshold

From the broker documentation: the FastStartFailoverThreshold property is ignored when the ObserverPingRetry and ObserverPingInterval properties are set. Detection then costs interval × (retries + 1), about a second at the minimum values.

An isolated primary runs its own clock

When a partition cuts the primary off from both the observer and the standby, redo generation stalls, V$DATABASE.FS_FAILOVER_STATUS goes to STALLED, and after FastStartFailoverThreshold seconds the primary aborts itself if FastStartFailoverPmyShutdown is TRUE. Two clocks, one open primary.

Transport layer

Where NET_TIMEOUT fits — and where it doesn't

NET_TIMEOUT is an attribute of LOG_ARCHIVE_DEST_n on the primary (broker property NetTimeout), and it is not a failover clock — it sets how many seconds LGWR blocks waiting for the standby to acknowledge redo shipped synchronously by the NSSn process, after which an error is logged, the redo transport session is terminated, and the destination is dropped. Neither the observer nor the target standby consults it when deciding to fail over; the observer's clocks run on a separate connection. Both it and FastStartFailoverThreshold default to 30 seconds, which is exactly why the two get confused — so both panels below span the same window, and the brace under each shows which clock is really running. Panel B additionally shortens NET_TIMEOUT to 10 s — shorter than the threshold, the exact configuration that looks like it should lose data — to show why it doesn't.

“NET_TIMEOUT 10 s, threshold 30 s — do I lose 20 s of redo?”

No. The worry assumes the primary starts committing alone at second 10 and the failover at second 30 discards those commits. But NET_TIMEOUT expiring only brings the primary to a decision point; whether it may actually continue without its standby is a separate check — and an isolated primary fails it.

Continuing alone requires a witness

With fast-start failover enabled, a MaxAvailability primary may drop to UNSYNCHRONIZED and keep committing only while it can still reach the observer or the target standby. In panel A the observer is reachable, so the primary carries on — and fast-start failover is simply disarmed until the standby resynchronizes. In panel B there is nobody to ask, so redo generation stalls: V$DATABASE.FS_FAILOVER_STATUS goes to STALLED.

Why the failover still loses nothing

Under SYNC a commit is not acknowledged until the standby holds its redo. Every transaction any client ever saw succeed is therefore already on the standby when it opens as the new primary. Commits issued during the stall never return — those sessions hang until the old primary aborts and get errors, not silent loss. A shorter NET_TIMEOUT only moves the moment the hanging starts.

In MaxPerformance (ASYNC) commits never wait for the standby, so NET_TIMEOUT never touches user sessions and never appears on the failover timeline; eligibility and data loss are bounded by FastStartFailoverLagLimit instead.

App readout

The service name is the switch

A role-based service exists only where the database currently runs as primary (srvctl add service -role PRIMARY, or a startup trigger checking DATABASE_ROLE). The standby’s listener is up and answers immediately — with ORA-12514, because nothing publishes app_svc there. That instant refusal is what makes a two-address string safe: the wrong database can never accept the connection.

The dead address is a toll booth

With LOAD_BALANCE off the client walks the list in order, so every traversal first pays min(TRANSPORT_CONNECT_TIMEOUT, CONNECT_TIMEOUT) waiting on the unreachable host — including every fresh connection after the failover, until the old primary is reinstated or the string reordered. LOAD_BALANCE = ON randomizes the order and halves the expected toll.

The default string retries exactly never

RETRY_COUNT defaults to 0: one traversal, then the error goes straight to the application. The budget must cover the whole outage: (RETRY_COUNT + 1) × traversal + RETRY_COUNT × RETRY_DELAY has to reach past the new primary’s open, with room to spare.

Short delays, many retries

Oracle’s HA guidance: a generous CONNECT_TIMEOUT (90 s in the documented example), a transport timeout around a second, and a short RETRY_DELAY with a high RETRY_COUNT, so that one of the traversals lands just after the service is republished. Its switchover sizing example budgets 100 × 3 s.

These parameters govern only the dial tone

Nothing here touches sessions that were already connected. They die by TCP rules — instant resets from a reachable host, long hangs across a partition — unless keepalives, SQLNET.RECV_TIMEOUT, or FAN break them faster; TAF or Application Continuity is what resumes their work afterwards. This panel models the pool’s new connections only.

When no failover is coming

If the readiness check above refuses the failover, the service is never republished; every traversal ends in ORA-12514, the string exhausts its budget, and the application stays down until a human intervenes. Retry parameters buy time, not availability.
